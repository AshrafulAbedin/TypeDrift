The global semiconductor shortage that began during the pandemic disrupted industries ranging from automotive manufacturing to consumer electronics and gaming, exposing the fragility of supply chains that the modern world had taken for granted for decades without question. Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company, which controls approximately fifty-five percent of the world's contract chip production, became a focal point of geopolitical tension as nations recognized that dependence on a single company in a strategically sensitive region posed unacceptable risks to national security. The extreme ultraviolet lithography machines required to manufacture the most advanced chips cost over two hundred million dollars each and are produced exclusively by the Dutch company ASML, creating a bottleneck that no amount of investment can quickly resolve. The United States responded with the CHIPS and Science Act, investing fifty-two billion dollars in domestic semiconductor manufacturing and research to reduce dependence on foreign suppliers. The European Union launched its own chip initiative and Japan partnered with TSMC to build fabrication plants domestically. Without semiconductors, the modern world simply ceases to function, as these tiny silicon chips power everything from smartphones and computers to automobiles, medical equipment, and military systems. The race to achieve chip independence has become one of the defining technological competitions of our century.
