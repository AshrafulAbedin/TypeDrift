The James Webb Space Telescope, a ten billion dollar engineering marvel representing two decades of development and international collaboration between NASA, the European Space Agency, and the Canadian Space Agency, sent back breathtaking images of distant galaxies, stellar nurseries, and exoplanet atmospheres that fundamentally changed our understanding of the universe we inhabit. The telescope's primary mirror, composed of eighteen gold-coated hexagonal segments spanning six and a half meters in diameter, collects infrared light with a sensitivity that allows it to peer deeper into the cosmos than any instrument humanity has ever created before. The first deep field image revealed thousands of galaxies in a patch of sky no larger than a grain of sand held at arm's length, each galaxy containing hundreds of billions of stars and potentially trillions of planets. Observations of the Carina Nebula revealed cosmic cliffs of gas and dust where new stars are being born, sculpted by intense ultraviolet radiation into formations of otherworldly beauty that no artist could have imagined. The telescope's ability to analyze exoplanet atmospheres by studying light passing through them during transits has opened a new chapter in the search for habitable worlds beyond our solar system. Webb's observations of the earliest galaxies challenged existing models of galaxy formation and reignited public fascination with space exploration.
